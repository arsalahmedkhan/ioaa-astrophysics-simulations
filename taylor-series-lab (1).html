1

What a derivative measures

The derivative f ′(a) is a rate: how fast f changes as x moves through a. On the graph it is the slope of the tangent. Step one unit to the right along the tangent and you rise by exactly f ′(a).

f(x) ≈ f(a) + f ′(a)·(x − a)This is the best straight-line copy of f near a. Notice which power of x appears: the first derivative multiplies (x − a) to the power 1.

That is the first link: the first derivative is the coefficient of the linear term. A line has a value and a slope, nothing more, so it can only copy those two things. To copy the bend as well, we will need x², and the second derivative.

The idea

A derivative turns “how fast is it changing?” into a number. That number is exactly what a straight line needs to match the curve at one point: its slope. Everything in this lab grows from that.

Try it

For x³, at which point is the tangent flat?

At a = 0, where f ′(0) = 3·0² = 0. The best line there is just y = 0, even though the curve keeps going.

For sin x at a = 0, what is the best line?

y = 0 + cos(0)·x = x. That is the small-angle rule sin x ≈ x.

2

Rate of change of rate of change

The derivative f ′ is itself a function, so it has its own rate of change: the second derivative f ″. That is the rate of change of the rate of change. Do it again and you get f ‴, the rate of change of the rate of change of the rate of change.

f → f ′ → f ″ → f ‴In motion: position → velocity → acceleration → jerk. Each panel’s tangent slope is the value plotted in the panel below it.

For x³ the chain is x³ → 3x² → 6x → 6 → 0: each step lowers the power by one, and after three steps it is a constant.

For sin x the chain never ends: sin → cos → −sin → −cos → sin → … The derivatives cycle with period four.

The idea

Higher derivatives describe finer and finer details of a curve. f ′ says which way it is heading, f ″ says how it is curving, and f ‴ says how that curving is changing. A Taylor series will use all of them at once.

Try it

For x³ at x = 1, give f, f ′, f ″ and f ‴.

1, 3, 6, 6. And f ⁗ = 0 everywhere, so x³ has only three non-zero derivatives.

For sin x at x = 0, list the first five derivatives.

sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1, −sin 0 = 0, −cos 0 = −1, then sin 0 = 0 again. The pattern 0, 1, 0, −1 repeats forever.

3

Point, slope, bend, twist: what each derivative looks like

Stand on a curve at one point a. Four numbers describe the curve there, each finer than the last. Add them one layer at a time:

numbernamewhat you see
f(a)pointhow high the curve is
f ′(a)slopewhich way it is heading: the tangent’s tilt
f ″(a)bendhow fast the slope is turning. + curves up like a cup, − curves down like a cap
f ‴(a)twisthow fast the bend is changing: the curve tightening or loosening as you move

Bend is the derivative of slope. Look at the short tangents just left and right of a. If the slope on the right is bigger than on the left, the curve is turning upward:

f ″(a) ≈ [f ′(a + h) − f ′(a − h)] / 2hThe change in slope divided by the distance between the two tangents. As h → 0 this becomes exactly f ″(a).

Twist is the derivative of bend. Measure the bend a little to the left and to the right. If it differs, the curve has a twist: f ‴(a) ≈ [f ″(a + h) − f ″(a − h)] / 2h.

The idea

Each derivative is the rate of change of the one before. Height changes at the rate slope, slope changes at the rate bend, and bend changes at the rate twist. Knowing all of them at one point tells you, more and more precisely, what the curve does nearby. That is exactly the information a Taylor series packs into its terms.

Try it

Pick x² and move a around. Why does the bend never change, and why is the twist always 0?

For x² the slope is 2x, which grows at the same rate everywhere, so f ″ = 2 at every point. A constant bend has no change, so f ‴ = 0. A parabola is the curve of pure, constant bending.

For sin x, where is the bend zero? What does the curve look like there?

At x = 0, ±π, …, where −sin x = 0. These are inflection points, where the curve switches from cap to cup and the tangent cuts straight through it.

4

Each derivative controls one power

Take a polynomial p(x) = c0 + c1x + c2x² + c3x³ and look only at x = 0.

x is pure slope. Its value at 0 is 0, its slope is 1, and it has no bend. So c1x sets p ′(0) = c1 and changes nothing else at 0.

x² is pure bend. At 0 it is flat (value 0, slope 0), but it curves upward with second derivative 2. So c2x² sets p ″(0) = 2c2 and leaves the value and slope alone.

x³ is pure twist. Its value, slope and bend at 0 are all zero. Only the third derivative, 6, survives. So c3x³ sets p ‴(0) = 6c3.

The idea

The first derivative belongs to x, the second to x², the third to x³, because xn is the simplest shape whose first non-zero derivative at 0 is the n-th. The n! appears because differentiating xn n times multiplies down n·(n − 1)·…·1.

Try it

Move only the c₂ slider. Does p(0) or p ′(0) change?

No. Both stay fixed: x² adds nothing to the value or slope at 0. Only the bend, p ″(0) = 2c₂, changes.

You want p ″(0) = 3. Which c₂ do you need?

c₂ = 3/2 = 1.5, because the second derivative of c₂x² is 2c₂. That factor of 2 is the 2! in the Taylor formula.

5

Choosing the values: why c₂ = f ″(0)/2

Say we want a polynomial p(x) = c0 + c1x + c2x² + c3x³ that copies f at 0. How do we pick each c? Differentiate p and set x = 0, one derivative at a time:

p(x)   = c0 + c1x + c2x² + c3x³   → at 0: c₀
p ′(x)  = c1 + 2c2x + 3c3x²   → at 0: c₁
p ″(x) = 2c2 + 6c3x   → at 0: 2·c₂
p ‴(x) = 6c3   → at 0: 6·c₃ Every term with an x in it vanishes at x = 0. Each line leaves exactly one coefficient, multiplied by the number the differentiation produced.

Now demand that p and f agree, line by line, and solve:

c0 = f(0),  c1 = f ′(0),  c2 = f ″(0)/2,  c3 = f ‴(0)/6The 2 and the 6 are 2! and 3!. In general, differentiating cₙxⁿ n times gives n!·cₙ, so cₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(0)/n!.

Try it yourself. c0 and c1 are already matched. Tune c2 and c3, or press the buttons to try the most common mistake, forgetting the factorial.

The idea

There is no guessing in a Taylor series. Each coefficient is forced: differentiate the polynomial n times, set x = 0, and only n!·cn survives. Making that equal to f(n)(0) leaves one possible value. Forget the n! and the polynomial bends or twists too hard.

Try it

For cos x, press “c₂ = f ″(0) ✗”. What goes wrong?

c₂ = −1 gives p ″(0) = 2·(−1) = −2, while cos ″(0) = −1. The parabola 1 − x² bends twice as hard as the cosine and drops away from it. With c₂ = −1/2 it hugs the curve.

For eˣ, which c₃ is correct, and why does c₃ = 1 look so wrong away from 0?

c₃ = 1/6, because 6c₃ must equal e⁰ = 1. With c₃ = 1 the cubic term is six times too strong, so p(x) curls away quickly on both sides.

6

Copying a curve, step by step

Now reverse the idea. Given a function f, choose the coefficients so the polynomial copies f’s derivatives at 0, one at a time:

cn = f(n)(0) / n!  ⟹  p(n)(0) = f(n)(0)Step 0 copies the value. Step 1 copies the slope. Step 2 copies the bend. Step 3 copies the twist… and each new term leaves the earlier matches untouched.

Why can polynomials copy smooth functions at all? Two curves with the same value, slope, bend, twist, … at a point cannot drift apart quickly near it. Matching more derivatives forces them together over a wider stretch. Polynomials are also the easiest functions there are: just multiplying and adding, which a calculator or a hand can do.

nf⁽ⁿ⁾(0)p⁽ⁿ⁾(0)term added

The idea

A Taylor polynomial is built by matching derivatives in order: first the value, then the slope, then the bend, then the twist… Each match is the job of exactly one power of x. That is the deep link between derivatives and the Taylor series.

Try it

For cos x, why do steps 1 and 3 add nothing?

cos ′(0) = −sin 0 = 0 and cos ‴(0) = sin 0 = 0. The curve has no slope or twist at 0, because it is symmetric, so only even powers appear: 1 − x²/2 + x⁴/24 − …

For eˣ, what is every coefficient?

Every derivative of eˣ is eˣ, which equals 1 at 0, so cₙ = 1/n!. That gives eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + …

7

Watch each term do its job

Now watch what adding one term actually does. For every term:

1. The new term appears on its own, as a purple curve.
2. The yellow shading shows the gap it is about to close.
3. The polynomial bends into its new shape and now matches one more derivative at 0.Zero terms are skipped quickly: when f⁽ⁿ⁾(0) = 0, that power is not needed.

The idea

Each term is a correction. The constant fixes the height, the x term fixes the slope, the x² term fixes the bend, the x³ term fixes the twist… and no correction disturbs the earlier ones at x = 0.

Try it

For eˣ, which term changes the curve the most near x = 1?

The early ones: 1 and x are worth 1 each at x = 1, x²/2 adds 0.5, and x³/6 adds 0.17. Each later term matters less and less, because n! grows so fast.

For sin x, why does the curve barely move when you add x⁹/9! near x = 1?

At x = 1 that term is only 1/362880 ≈ 0.000003. Its effect shows up further out, where x⁹ is large.

The recipe: build any series, row by row

Here is the whole method as a worksheet. Every Taylor series is built with the same four moves, repeated for n = 0, 1, 2, …

1. Differentiate: find f(n)(x), the derivative of the row above.
2. Evaluate at the centre: f(n)(a).
3. Divide by n!:  cn = f(n)(a) / n!
4. Write the term: cn·(x − a)n, and add it on.Press “Next step” to do one move at a time. The graph grows as each term is added. Change the centre a to expand around a different point.
n1. f⁽ⁿ⁾(x)2. f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)3. ÷ n!4. term

The idea

Building a series is mechanical: keep differentiating, plug in the centre, divide by the factorial, attach the power. Patterns jump out quickly: 1/n! for eˣ, alternating odd powers for sin, (−1)n+1/n for ln(1 + x). Once you see the pattern you can write the whole infinite series.

Try it

Build √(1 + x) at a = 0 up to x². Use it to estimate √1.1.

Rows: 1, then ½, then −¼ ÷ 2 = −1/8. So √(1 + x) ≈ 1 + x/2 − x²/8. At x = 0.1: 1 + 0.05 − 0.00125 = 1.04875. The true value is 1.04881.

Build sin x around a = π/2 (slide a to 1.57). Which terms survive?

sin(π/2) = 1, cos(π/2) = 0, −sin(π/2) = −1, … so only even powers survive: 1 − (x − π/2)²/2 + (x − π/2)⁴/24 − … This is the cosine series shifted, because sin x = cos(x − π/2).

9

Example 1: x³, the series that stops

The general Taylor series works around any point a, using powers of (x − a):

f(x) = f(a) + f ′(a)(x − a) + f ″(a)/2! (x − a)² + f ‴(a)/3! (x − a)³ + …

For f(x) = x³ the derivatives are a³, 3a², 6a, 6, and then 0 forever. So the series has only four terms, and with all four it is exact everywhere.

Worked example, a = 1.
f(1) = 1, f ′(1) = 3, f ″(1) = 6, f ‴(1) = 6
x³ = 1 + 3(x − 1) + (6/2!)(x − 1)² + (6/3!)(x − 1)³
   = 1 + 3(x − 1) + 3(x − 1)² + (x − 1)³
Expand: (x³ − 3x² + 3x − 1) + (3x² − 6x + 3) + (3x − 3) + 1 = x³ ✓

The idea

For a polynomial, the Taylor series is just the same polynomial rewritten around a new centre. It ends because high enough derivatives are zero. That makes it a good check that the formula really rebuilds the function.

Try it

With a = 1 and n = 1, what is the approximation at x = 1.1? How close is it?

1 + 3(0.1) = 1.3. The true value is 1.331, so the error is 0.031. With n = 2: 1.3 + 3(0.01) = 1.33, an error of only 0.001.

What happens if you move a to 0?

f(0) = f ′(0) = f ″(0) = 0, so the first three terms vanish and the series is just (6/3!)x³ = x³.

10

Example 2: sin x, the series that never stops

At 0 the derivatives of sin follow the cycle 0, 1, 0, −1, 0, 1, … The even ones vanish, and the odd ones alternate in sign. Dividing by n! gives

sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − x⁷/7! + …Only odd powers appear, because sin is an odd function: sin(−x) = −sin x.

Unlike x³, this never stops: every fourth derivative is back to sin itself. Each extra term extends the stretch where the polynomial is accurate. The green band marks where the error is below 0.01.

The idea

An infinite series can equal a function exactly. Every partial sum is a polynomial, and adding more terms makes it match over any range you choose. For sin x the series works for every x, because n! eventually beats any power xn.

Try it

At x = π/2, how many terms get you within 0.001 of 1?

Up to x⁷ (four terms): 0.99984. With three terms, up to x⁵, you get 1.0045, which is not close enough.

Why does a degree-25 polynomial still fail far out, say at x = 20?

Any polynomial eventually shoots off to ±∞, while sin stays between −1 and 1. Each finite piece is only good up to some distance, but that distance grows with every term.

11

How good is it? Error and convergence

Stopping after degree N leaves a remainder. It behaves like the first term you left out:

|RN(x)| ≤ M · |x|N+1 / (N + 1)!Here M is the largest size of the (N+1)-th derivative between 0 and x. Small x and large N make the error tiny.

Some series work for every x (eˣ, sin, cos). Others only work inside a radius of convergence. For ln(1 + x) and 1/(1 − x) that radius is 1. Outside it, adding terms makes things worse. Watch the error chart climb.

The idea

A Taylor series is local knowledge. It sees only the derivatives at one point. For eˣ or sin that is enough to rebuild the whole function. For ln(1 + x), the trouble at x = −1 limits how far the series can see in either direction.

Try it

For ln(1 + x), set x = 0.5 and then x = 1.5. What does the error chart do as N grows?

At 0.5 the error falls steadily (N = 10 is within 0.00003). At 1.5 it rises: the terms 1.5ⁿ/n grow, so the series diverges.

For eˣ at x = 1, how many terms give e to three decimals?

Up to degree 6: 2.71806, against e = 2.71828. Degree 5 gives 2.7167, which is not quite there.

12

Why we need it

Physics rarely needs the whole series, only the first term or two. Keeping the leading terms turns hard formulas into simple ones and shows what really matters for small quantities. Calculators also compute sin, cos and exp with short polynomials like these.

The idea

“Expand to first order” is one of the most useful moves in physics. Newton’s ½mv² is the first Taylor term of Einstein’s energy. The Doppler rule z ≈ v/c is the first term of the relativistic formula. Small angles make trigonometry linear.

Try it

Use z ≈ v/c for a galaxy at v = 0.1c. How wrong is it?

The exact relativistic z = √(1.1/0.9) − 1 = 0.1055, so z ≈ 0.1 is about 5% low. At 0.01c the error is only 0.5%.

A planet transit dims a star by 1%. What is the change in magnitude?

Δm = −2.5 log₁₀(1 − 0.01) ≈ 1.086 × 0.01 = 0.0109 mag. The exact value is 0.01091, which is very close.

Summary

Derivative

f ′(a): the rate of change at aThe slope of the best straight line, which gives the coefficient of (x − a).

Higher derivatives

f ″, f ‴, …The rate of change of the rate of change… For motion: velocity, acceleration, jerk.

Powers and derivatives

dⁿ/dxⁿ xⁿ = n!xⁿ is the only power with a non-zero n-th derivative at 0. That is why the n-th derivative pairs with xⁿ.

Point, slope, bend, twist

f(a), f ′(a), f ″(a), f ‴(a)Height, direction, how fast the slope turns, how fast the bend changes. Each is the rate of change of the one before.

Choosing coefficients

n!·cₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(0) ⟹ cₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(0)/n!Differentiate p n times and set x = 0: only n!·cₙ survives.

The recipe

Differentiate → evaluate at a → divide by n! → multiply by (x − a)ⁿ → add. Repeat and look for the pattern.

Taylor series

f(x) = Σ f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n! · (x − a)ⁿA polynomial built to match every derivative at a.

Maclaurin series (a = 0)

eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − …cos x = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − …

Radius of convergence
