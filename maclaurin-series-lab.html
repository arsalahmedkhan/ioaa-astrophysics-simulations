Derivatives at zero → a whole function

Everything from one point.

Know a function’s value, slope, bend, twist, … at x = 0, and you can rebuild it everywhere with nothing but powers of x. We build that idea one derivative at a time, then put it together into the Maclaurin series.

1

What a derivative does

A derivative answers one question: how fast is this changing, right here? Pick a point on the curve and a second point a step h away. The slope between them is rise ÷ run. Now shrink h. The line through the two points swings round and settles on the tangent, and its slope settles on one number: the derivative.

f ′(x) = limh→0 [f(x + h) − f(x)] / hA derivative turns a curve into a new function: the slope at every point. Doing it again and again gives f ″, f ‴, … Each one carries finer information about the shape.

We will follow three examples all the way through: sin x (a wave), x³ (a polynomial) and eˣ (growth).

The idea

The derivative is the rate of change at an instant: the slope the secant lines settle on as the step shrinks to nothing.

Try it

For x³ at x = 1, what slope do the secants approach?

3x² = 3. With h = 1 the secant slope is (8 − 1)/1 = 7. With h = 0.1 it is 3.31, closing in on 3.

For eˣ, compare the slope with the height. What do you notice?

They are equal everywhere: the derivative of eˣ is eˣ. The steepness is always the same as the value.

First derivative: slope

f ′(x) tells you which way the curve is heading and how steeply. Where it is positive the curve rises, where it is negative it falls, and where it is zero the curve is momentarily flat.

f(x)f ′(x)f ′(0)what it says at 0
sin xcos x1rising at 45°
x³3x²0perfectly flat
eˣeˣ1rising, as steep as it is high
y = f(0) + f ′(0)·xThe value and the first derivative at 0 give the best straight line through the origin point. The first derivative is the coefficient of x.
3

Second derivative: bend

f ″(x) is the slope of the slope: how fast the tangent is turning. Positive means the curve bends up like a cup. Negative means it bends down like a cap. Zero means it is not bending there (an inflection point).

f(x)f ″(x)f ″(0)at 0
sin x−sin x0switching from cup to cap
x³6x0switching from cap to cup
eˣeˣ1a gentle cup
y = f(0) + f ′(0)x + f ″(0)/2·x²The best parabola at 0. The bend needs x², the simplest curve that bends, and the ½ comes from (x²)″ = 2. Chapter 6 shows why.

Third derivative: twist

f ‴(x) is the slope of the bend: how fast the bending itself changes as you move along. On the lower graph it is the slope of the f ″ curve, shown by the gold tangent.

f(x)f ‴(x)f ‴(0)at 0
sin x−cos x−1the bend is decreasing: cup → cap
x³66the bend grows steadily: cap → cup
eˣeˣ1the bend is slowly increasing
… + f ‴(0)/6·x³The twist needs x³, and the 6 is 3! = 3·2·1 because (x³)‴ = 6. For x³ itself this gives 6/6·x³ = x³, which is exact.

Notice the pattern: sin x keeps cycling (sin → cos → −sin → −cos → sin), x³ runs out (6 → 0 → 0 …), and eˣ never changes.

Chapters 2–4 together

Value, slope, bend, twist: each is the rate of change of the one before. At x = 0 each one is a single number, and each number needs exactly one power of x to carry it: 1, x, x², x³. That pairing is the whole idea of the Maclaurin series.

Try it

For sin x, list f(0), f ′(0), f ″(0), f ‴(0).

0, 1, 0, −1. Then the cycle repeats: 0, 1, 0, −1, …

Why is the twist of x³ the same everywhere?

f ″ = 6x is a straight line, and its slope is always 6. The bend grows at a constant rate.

5

Building with polynomials

Polynomials are the simplest functions there are: just multiply and add. The plan is to build our function out of these building blocks:

p(x) = a0 + a1x + a2x² + a3x³ + a4x⁴ + …The blocks 1, x, x², x³, x⁴ are fixed. The only freedom is the amounts a₀, a₁, a₂, … Choosing them well is the whole problem.

Try it by hand. Pick a target and tune the five amounts to make the yellow curve match the blue one inside the green band, −1 ≤ x ≤ 1. It is surprisingly hard to guess. The next chapter shows there is a formula that gets every amount exactly right.

1xx²x³x⁴your p(x)

The idea

Any polynomial is a mix of powers of x. To copy a function we need the right mix. Guessing works badly, and it gets harder with every block you add. We need a rule that tells us each amount directly.

Try it

For eˣ, which a₀ and a₁ are easy to guess?

a₀ = 1, because e⁰ = 1 (the curve crosses at height 1). a₁ = 1, because the slope at 0 is 1. The rest are harder: they turn out to be 1/2, 1/6, 1/24.

For sin x, why should a₀, a₂ and a₄ all be 0?

sin is odd: sin(−x) = −sin x. The blocks 1, x², x⁴ are even (symmetric), so any amount of them would spoil that symmetry. Only odd powers can appear.

6

Why derivatives make it exact

Here is the trick. Differentiate the polynomial again and again, and look only at x = 0. Each time you differentiate, every power comes down as a multiplier and drops by one: x³ → 3x² → 6x → 6. The constant at the front falls off.

Then set x = 0. Every term that still has an x becomes 0. Exactly one term survives on each row: the one that has just become a constant.

p(n)(0) = n!·an  ⟹  set it equal to f(n)(0):  an = f(n)(0) / n!So each amount is the function’s n-th derivative at 0, divided by the n! that differentiating produced. Matching the derivatives fixes every amount exactly. No guessing.
The derivative staircase

The idea

Differentiating n times and setting x = 0 picks out one coefficient, multiplied by n!. That is why the derivatives of f at 0, divided by factorials, are exactly the right amounts of each building block.

Try it

After three steps, what survives at x = 0, and what does it equal for sin x?

p‴(0) = 6a₃. For sin, f ‴(0) = −cos 0 = −1, so a₃ = −1/6.

Why does a₄x⁴ give 24a₄ after four steps?

4 → 4·3 → 4·3·2 → 4·3·2·1 = 24 = 4!. Each step brings down the current power.

7

The Maclaurin series

Put every amount in place and keep going forever. That is the Maclaurin series: the Taylor series centred at 0.

f(x) = f(0) + f ′(0)x + f ″(0)/2! x² + f ‴(0)/3! x³ + … = Σ f(n)(0)/n! · xn
Our three examples, finished:
sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − x⁷/7! + …  (the derivatives at 0 cycle 0, 1, 0, −1)
x³ = 0 + 0·x + 0·x² + (6/3!)x³ = x³  (it stops: the series is the polynomial itself)
eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + x⁴/4! + …  (every derivative at 0 is 1)

The idea

Each term is a correction. The constant fixes the height, the x term fixes the slope, the x² term fixes the bend, the x³ term fixes the twist… and every correction leaves the earlier ones untouched at x = 0. Step by step, the polynomial agrees with the function over a wider and wider stretch.

Try it

For x³ − 2x + 1, how many terms until it is exact?

Four (n = 0 to 3): 1, −2x, 0·x², x³. After that every derivative is 0, so the polynomial is the function itself.

For cos x, which terms get skipped, and why?

All the odd ones (x, x³, x⁵, …). cos is symmetric, so its odd derivatives at 0 (−sin 0, sin 0, …) are all 0.
