The mathematics of adding up

Infinitely many, infinitely thin.

An integral adds up infinitely many tiny pieces: thin rectangles under a curve, small steps of a journey, slivers of area swept by a planet. Build it from rectangles, discover the Fundamental Theorem, and use it on orbits.

Area from rectangles

There is no formula for the area under a curve, but there is one for rectangles. Slice [a, b] into n strips of width Δx, give each a height taken from the curve, and add them up. This is a Riemann sum.

Sn = f(x1)Δx + f(x2)Δx + … + f(xn)ΔxWhere each height is sampled (left edge, right edge, middle) changes the estimate. As n → ∞ every choice gives the same limit: the integral.

Gold strips overestimate their slice and teal strips underestimate it. Watch both kinds of error vanish as the strips get thinner.

The sample point can be anywhere in its strip, even chosen at random. For a continuous function every choice gives the same limit. That is what makes the integral well defined.

The idea

An integral is the limit of sums of thin strips. Left and right sums close in like 1/n: double n and the error halves. Midpoint and trapezoid sums close in like 1/n²: double n and the error drops four times. That is why numerical methods prefer them.

Try it

With Left sums on x², is the estimate too big or too small? Why?

Too small. x² rises on [0, 2], so the left edge is the lowest point of each strip and every rectangle sits below the curve. With n = 6 the sum is 2.037 against the true 8/3 ≈ 2.667.

Use Midpoint with n = 10 on sin x. How close do you get to 2?

2.00825, an error of only 0.008. The left sum with n = 10 is off by about 0.016, twice as much.

On the error chart (log scales), what slope do the Left dots follow? And the Midpoint dots?

Slope −1 for Left (error ∝ 1/n) and slope −2 for Midpoint (error ∝ 1/n²). On log scales a power law is a straight line, and its slope is the power.

A Riemann sum, term by term

Here is what the Σ symbol is actually doing. Every Riemann sum has three ingredients:

Σi=1n f(xi*) · ΔxΣ, i = 1 to n: add up one term per strip.  f(xᵢ*): the height of strip i, taken at its sample point xᵢ*.  Δx = (b − a)/n: the width of every strip.

The strips sit on a partition a = x0 < x1 < … < xn = b with xi = a + i·Δx. Press Add them up to watch each term computed and added in turn, then see how close the total lands to the exact area.

The idea

A Riemann sum is ordinary arithmetic: multiply each height by the width and add. The only new idea is the limit. Make the partition finer and finer, and the total settles on one number, the definite integral.

Try it

With n = 4 Right sums on x², write out the four terms.

(0.5)²·0.5 + (1)²·0.5 + (1.5)²·0.5 + (2)²·0.5 = 0.125 + 0.5 + 1.125 + 2 = 3.75. The exact area is 8/3 ≈ 2.667.

Which strip contributes the most, and why?

The last one. x² is largest at the right end, so that strip is the tallest. For a rising function the right sum overestimates, because every height is taken at the top of its strip.

3

Signed area

Where f dips below the axis, its strips have negative height. So the integral counts area above the axis as positive and area below it as negative.

∫ab f dx = (area above) − (area below)To get the total area, add the pieces with ∫|f| dx instead. Swapping a and b flips the sign of the integral.

The idea

The integral measures net accumulation. For a velocity, that is net displacement. Driving 5 km forward and 5 km back gives 0, even though you covered 10 km.

Try it

What is ∫ sin x dx from 0 to 2π? What is the total area?

The integral is 0: the hump above cancels the hump below. The total area is 2 + 2 = 4.

Press ⇄ Swap a and b. What happens?

The integral changes sign: ∫ₐᵇ f = −∫ᵦᵃ f. The strips are the same, but each Δx is now negative.

4

Fundamental Theorem I: area has slope f

Let the right edge move. The area so far, A(x) = ∫0x f(t) dt, becomes a new function, the accumulation function.

Push x on by a tiny dx. The area grows by one thin strip of height f(x), so dA ≈ f(x)·dx. In other words, the slope of A is f.

d/dx ∫0x f(t) dt = f(x)Integration and differentiation undo each other: the area function is an antiderivative of f. Part II (next chapter) turns this into a way to compute areas.

More carefully: the new strip is squeezed between rectangles of height min f and max f on [x, x + dx]. Divide by dx, and both bounds tend to f(x) because f is continuous. That is the whole proof.

area so farnew strip f(x)·dxA(x)Drag on the top graph

The idea

Two ideas that look unrelated, slopes and areas, are inverse operations. Areas that would take infinitely many rectangles can be found by asking one question: which function has f as its derivative?

Try it

For cos x, where is A(x) largest?

At x = π/2, where cos x turns negative. After that each new strip subtracts area. A(π/2) = sin(π/2) = 1.

Why does the slope of A always equal f(x)?

Adding a strip of width dx adds about f(x)·dx of area, so ΔA/dx → f(x) as dx → 0.

5

Fundamental Theorem II: why F(b) − F(a)

Suppose F ′ = f. Cut [a, b] into n steps. Across step i, F rises by ΔFi = F(xi) − F(xi−1).

ΔF1 + ΔF2 + … + ΔFn = F(b) − F(a)Telescoping: every in-between value is added once and subtracted once, so only the two ends survive.

The mean value theorem says each rise equals slope × run at some point ci inside the step: ΔFi = F ′(ci)·Δx = f(ci)·Δx. That is exactly the area of rectangle i. So

Σ f(ci)Δx = F(b) − F(a)  for every n  ⟹  ∫ab f dx = F(b) − F(a)Each coloured rectangle on the top graph has the same area as the matching coloured rise on the bottom graph.
Worked examples.
∫02 x² dx = [x³/3]02 = 8/3
∫0π sin x dx = [−cos x]0π = 1 − (−1) = 2
∫1e dx/x = [ln x]1e = 1
Escaping a planet: the work to lift mass m from radius R to infinity is ∫R∞ GMm/r² dr = [−GMm/r]R∞ = GMm/R. Setting ½mv² = GMm/R gives the escape speed v = √(2GM/R).

The idea

The total change of F is the sum of its small changes, and each small change is a rate times a width. Add infinitely many and you get the integral of the rate. Part I says the area function’s slope is f. Part II says adding up f gives the total change of any antiderivative.

Try it

With mean-value points, why is the sum exact even for n = 1?

The mean value theorem gives one point c with f(c)·(b − a) = F(b) − F(a): a single rectangle with exactly the right area. Switch to Left points and the match breaks until n is large.

Use the theorem to find ∫₁⁴ dx/x.

F(x) = ln x, so the integral is ln 4 − ln 1 = ln 4 ≈ 1.386. Pick 1/x and set b = 4 to check.

6

Distance from speed

For a short time dt, speed is nearly constant, so the distance covered is v·dt: a thin strip under the velocity graph. Add the strips and the area under v(t) is the displacement.

x(t) − x(0) = ∫0t v(s) dsArea below the axis means moving backwards. In the same way, ∫ a dt is the change in velocity.

The idea

Integrals turn rates into totals: speed into distance, power into energy, flow into volume. For a probe whose speed is logged over time, the integral of those readings tells you how far it has travelled.

Try it

For “Cruise, then brake”, how far does it travel in total?

A rectangle plus a triangle: 12 × 4 + ½ × 12 × 3 = 48 + 18 = 66 m.

For “Back and forth”, compare displacement and distance over one full swing (t = 0 to 2π).

The displacement is 0: the areas above and below cancel. The distance is ∫|v| dt = 4 × 3 = 12 m.

7

Kepler’s equal areas

Kepler’s second law says a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Near the star it moves fast and its slices are short and wide. Far away it moves slowly and its slices are long and thin.

Each slice is a fan of thin triangles, each with area ½r²dθ. The slice’s area is the integral of those triangles:

A = ½ ∫ r(θ)² dθ,    dA/dt = ½r²dθ/dt = constantThe constant is half the angular momentum per unit mass, so the law is conservation of angular momentum in disguise.

The idea

Integrals measure shapes that no ruler can, like the curved slices of an ellipse. Computing ½∫r²dθ for each month-long slice gives the same number every time: one twelfth of the ellipse’s area, πab/12.

Try it

Set e = 0.6. Where does the planet move fastest, and by how much?

At perihelion. The speed ratio is (1 + e)/(1 − e) = 4, so the planet moves 4× faster at perihelion than at aphelion.

Set e = 0. What do the slices look like?

Twelve identical 30° wedges. For a circle, equal times mean equal angles, as you would expect.
